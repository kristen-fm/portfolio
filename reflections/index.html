Technical Upskilling: Are We Walking in Circles Without a Rhetorical Compass?

In this reflective essay, I examine a persistent challenge I encountered while designing and facilitating technical upskilling programs for software engineers at Pluralsight: learners expressed enthusiasm for professional development but frequently struggled to participate and persist. Drawing on learner feedback and my own experiences as an educator, I explore why employees often feel guilty about prioritizing learning over their everyday work responsibilities.

I argue that improving learner engagement requires more than designing effective learning experiences; it also requires helping learners understand why the learning matters and inviting them into the process. Drawing on Lloyd Bitzer’s (1968) concept of the rhetorical situation and Barbara Schneider’s (2008) application of rhetorical theory to faculty professional development, I suggest that educators and organizational leaders share responsibility for establishing a compelling rationale for learning.

I include this essay in my portfolio because it demonstrates how I use scholarly theory to critically examine challenges in my professional practice and reconsider my approach to designing and facilitating adult learning experiences.

References

Bitzer, L. F. (1968). The rhetorical situation. Philosophy & Rhetoric, 1(1), 1–14.

Schneider, B. (2008). The rhetorical situation: Examining the framing of professional development. Pedagogy, 8(3), 509–522. https://doi.org/10.1215/15314200-2008-009

Learn How to Code: Insights From the L2 Motivational Self System

In this essay, I explore how research on motivation in second language acquisition might help explain why some adults persist through the challenges of learning to code, whereas others give up. Drawing on Zoltán Dörnyei’s (2005) L2 Motivational Self System, I introduce the concepts of the Ideal L2 Self and Ought-to L2 Self and consider how these motivational constructs might apply to aspiring software developers.

My goal was to make research on language learning motivation accessible and relevant to adults learning programming languages, while offering practical strategies for understanding and sustaining their own motivation. Through examples and reflective exercises, I encourage learners to envision their future selves and consider how those imagined identities might influence their persistence.

I include this essay in my portfolio because it demonstrates how I draw on scholarship from one discipline, second language acquisition, to explore questions about adult learning in another context. It also illustrates my interest in connecting theory to practice while acknowledging the limits of applying a theoretical framework beyond the context in which it has been empirically studied.

Reference

Dörnyei, Z. (2005). The psychology of the language learner: Individual differences in second language acquisition. Lawrence Erlbaum.
