“My code is shit”: Negative automatic thoughts and outcomes of a behavioral experiment for code review anxiety

This research article reports on a mixed-methods intervention study on mitigating code review anxiety. At the time, research had not yet examined intervention strategies for mitigating software practitioners’ code review anxiety during real-world code review. To fill this lacuna, my co-authors and I developed and assessed the effectiveness of a guided, behavioral toolkit to be used during code reviews. The toolkit teaches software practitioners to identify and challenge negative automatic thoughts that arise during code review, and to replace them with thoughts that support learning and growth.

I include this work here both to highlight my experience in conducting empirical research in workplace contexts, and because it connects directly to my applied practice. Because the study findings indicated that the toolkit reduced code review anxiety and decreased the believability of negative automatic thoughts, Dr. Carol S. Lee and I developed and shared with the software practitioner community the Code Review Anxiety Workbook as a free Internet resource.

Together, I hope the two artifacts demonstrate my burgeoning abilities as a scholar-practitioner, my commitment to evaluating the efficacy of educational interventions, and my penchant for disseminating research findings in ways that benefit relevant learning communities.

Lee, C. S., Hicks, C. M., & Foster-Marks, K. L. (2024). “My code is shit”: Negative automatic thoughts and outcomes of a behavioral experiment for code review anxiety. PsyArXiv. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/hz3et

Microinclusions: a small way to increase sense of belonging and fit on tech teams

A commentary I wrote for the Developer Success Lab’s Developer Science Review, responding to Muragishi et al.’s (2023) research on microinclusions — the small acts of interpersonal treatment that signal whether others are receptive to and supportive of our contributions to shared work. Across four experiments, the authors found these acts especially bolster a sense of fit for women in technology, whose contributions are too often marginalized.

I include it because it connects the empirical research to my own lived experience on tech teams and issues a practical challenge: to notice, and to practice, microinclusions in our everyday work.
