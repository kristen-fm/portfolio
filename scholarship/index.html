October 2024 · Research Article

“My code is shit”: Negative automatic thoughts and outcomes of a behavioral experiment for code review anxiety

This research article (Lee et al., 2024) reports on a mixed-methods intervention study on mitigating code review anxiety. At the time, research had not yet examined intervention strategies for mitigating software practitioners’ code review anxiety during real-world code review. To fill this lacuna, my co-authors and I developed and assessed the effectiveness of a guided, behavioral toolkit to be used during code reviews. The toolkit teaches software practitioners to identify and challenge negative automatic thoughts that arise during code review, and to replace them with thoughts that support learning and growth.

I include this work here both to highlight my experience in conducting empirical research in workplace contexts, and because it connects directly to my applied practice. Because the study findings indicated that the toolkit reduced code review anxiety and decreased the believability of negative automatic thoughts, Dr. Carol S. Lee and I developed and shared with the software practitioner community the Code Review Anxiety Workbook as a free Internet resource.

Together, I hope the two artifacts demonstrate my burgeoning abilities as a scholar-practitioner, my commitment to evaluating the efficacy of educational interventions, and my penchant for disseminating research findings in ways that benefit relevant learning communities. More broadly, they reflect my interest in conducting research within the professional communities to which I belong, using the knowledge generated through empirical inquiry to inform educational practice and contribute to those communities (Stevens-Wood & Attfield, 2024).

July 2024 · Commentary

Microinclusions: a small way to increase sense of belonging and fit on tech teams

In this commentary I wrote for the Developer Success Lab’s Developer Science Review, I respond to Muragishi et al.’s (2024) research on microinclusions on technology teams. Microinclusions are acts that “convey a receptivity and supportiveness to one’s contributions” (p. 431). Across four experiments, the authors found these acts can serve to bolster a sense of fit for women in technology, whose value and team contributions have been historically minimized.

Drawing on my own experiences working as a software engineer on tech teams, I reflect on the significance of these seemingly small interpersonal behaviors and encourage readers to consider how their own actions might influence their colleagues’ sense of belonging and fit. I also consider the potential for well-intentioned acts of inclusion to inadvertently exclude others, highlighting the importance of attending to individual differences and the social contexts in which these interactions occur.

This commentary reflects my interest in connecting empirical research to professional practice, particularly by encouraging practitioners to critically reflect on their own experiences, beliefs, and behaviors. It also reflects my developing identity as a scholar-practitioner, drawing on my experience within the software engineering community to help bridge the gap between academic research and professional practice (Stevens-Wood & Attfield, 2024).
