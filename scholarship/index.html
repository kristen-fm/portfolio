Research & Scholarship

My research interests center on adult learning, particularly motivation and participation, self-directed learning, learner beliefs, and learning in workplace contexts. I draw primarily on the adult education and learning sciences literatures, including work on educational participation, metacognition, self-regulated and self-directed learning, and sociocultural perspectives on learning. I am especially interested in how these bodies of research can inform the design of learning environments that support sustained engagement, autonomy, and meaningful learning.

Preprint · 2024

“My code is shit”: Negative automatic thoughts and outcomes of a behavioral experiment for code review anxiety

This co-authored preprint reports a study on mitigating code review anxiety — a common experience that interrupts important processes in software development, including improving code quality, social learning, and creative problem-solving. Because prior research had not yet examined intervention strategies for use during a real-world code review, the study developed and assessed a guided behavioral experiment toolkit. The findings indicated that the toolkit reduced code review anxiety and decreased the believability of negative automatic thoughts (NATs). A qualitative analysis further showed that participants were most likely to engage in the thinking trap of catastrophizing, and that they most frequently challenged their NATs by boosting their self-efficacy.

I include this work because it grounds my interest in learner beliefs and learning in workplace contexts in an empirical, real-world study, and because it connects directly to my applied learning-design practice: the behavioral experiment examined here shares its cognitive-behavioral foundation with the Code Review Anxiety Workbook featured on my Learning Design page. Together the two artifacts show a praxis loop — research informing design, and design extending research into practice.

Lee, C. S., Hicks, C. M., & Foster-Marks, K. L. (2024). “My code is shit”: Negative automatic thoughts and outcomes of a behavioral experiment for code review anxiety. PsyArXiv. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/hz3et

July 2024 · Commentary

Microinclusions: a small way to increase sense of belonging and fit on tech teams

A commentary I wrote for the Developer Success Lab’s Developer Science Review, responding to Muragishi et al.’s (2023) research on microinclusions — the small acts of interpersonal treatment that signal whether others are receptive to and supportive of our contributions to shared work. Across four experiments, the authors found these acts especially bolster a sense of fit for women in technology, whose contributions are too often marginalized. I include it because it connects the empirical research to my own lived experience on tech teams and issues a practical challenge: to notice, and to practice, microinclusions in our everyday work.
