    Kathie Sarachild & Consciousness-Raising

    “In our groups, let’s share our feelings and pool them. Let’s let ourselves go and see where our feelings lead us. Our feelings will lead us to ideas and then to actions.”

    — Kathie Sarachild, 1970

    Historical Context

    Lead sentence placeholder. Supporting text will appear here, framing what this section covers and why it matters.

    "Consciousness-raising as a method for developing epistemically well-founded claims about women's oppression arose from a range of women's discussion groups in the 1970s. By 1973, it is estimated that over 100,000 women participated in consciousness-raising groups in the U.S. alone, and many such groups had also arisen overseas" (Engelstad, 2026, p. 3).


    "At the time, sexism and gendered oppression were not well known or understood phenomena. Instead, women were generally seen as inherently submissive, weka irrational, emotional, and caring, and therefore best suited for domestic labour and submission to a male head of household. Individual women would often take it as a personal failure if they struggled to conform to these expectations" (Engelstad, 2026, p. 3).


    Kathie Sarachild is a writer, radical feminist, and the person most closely associated with Consciousness-Raising Groups.


    The exact origins of CR can’t be attributed to a single person, and in fact, historical accounts trace elements of the practice to labor and Black liberation organizing, in which participants examined their experiences collectively as a means of developing political understanding.


    But Sarachild played an important role in articulating and disseminating the method. Having participated in the Civil Rights Movement, she was familiar with the political possibilities of sharing grievances and collective experiences. She and the New York Radical Women were instrumental in popularizing the practice.

    Consciousness-Raising

    Lead sentence placeholder. Supporting text will appear here, framing what this section covers and why it matters.

    The term consciousness-raising emerged with the New York Radical Women. According to accounts of the group’s early meeting, Anne Forer suggested that members describe ways they had experienced oppression as women because she wanted to “raise” her own consciousness about women’s oppression.

    Sarachild subsequently adopted consciousness-raising as the name for the social practice. In 1968, she distributed the [genre?] A Program for Feminist Consciousness-Raising at a national women’s liberation conference, and she later provided a more extensive explanation of the practice in the article “Consciousness-Raising: A Radical Weapon.”

    small groups of women gathered together to discuss their experiences with marriage, work, sexuality, motherhood, housework, and other aspects of their lives.

    CR was not simply a discussion technique, but a distinctive form of collective knowledge production.

    In practice, CR was meant to begin with women’s real, lived experiences rather than existing theory of women’s oppression, which had been critiqued by women as having been produced by men, their oppressors.


    Members of CR groups typically considered a particular subject and took turns describing their own experiences with it. Many groups had explicit rules that asked them to wait to debate, evaluate, or theorize on those experiences, instead allowing participants the space to articulate and establish what had actually happened in their lives. The group could then compare accounts, and look for recurring patterns across what they previously thought had been individual, idiosyncratic experiences.

    CR was never meant to end with personal disclosure, but rather provide the empirical material from which an explanation could be constructed, and then ultimately acted upon. Things that were found to have been experienced by many were considered a sort of knowledge.


    Fundamentally, CR was considered a method for investigating women’s social conditions, developing feminist theory from lived experience, and using the knowledge produced through that inquiry as a basis for political action.

    Consider the distribution of household labor.


    A woman who performs substantially more domestic work than her husband might initially explain the situation through the particular characteristics of her marriage. Perhaps she:

    • married an inconsiderate man,
    • failed to negotiate responsibilities effectively, or
    • is uniquely bothered by household disorder.

    These explanations cause her to live quietly with her anger, disappointment, resentment, and whatever other negative emotions arise from her situation.


    But consider that this woman learns that many other women are living under similar arrangements, and are equally discontent with their situations. In this way, alternative explanations become plausible. Rather than asking only why one husband behaves in a particular way, participants can ask why domestic labor is repeatedly assigned to women across different households. An apparently private relationship problem then becomes evidence for investigating a broader gendered social arrangement.

    Significance of CR

    What was the social, intellectual, and theoretical significance of CR? Its force came from a simple but radical move: treating women’s own lived experience as evidence worth investigating.

    CR encouraged women to take seriously their own lived experiences as evidence. Sarachild explicitly objected to the assumption that established authorities already possessed the conceptual and theoretical tools necessary to explain women’s experiences. She argued that instead, women could investigate their own conditions collectively and develop theory from what they discovered.


    Consciousness-raising was therefore simultaneously experiential and analytical. Personal experiences constituted a starting point for inquiry, but individual interpretation was not necessarily its endpoint. Experiences became especially politically revealing when compared, questioned, and situated alongside the experiences of other women.


    CR surfaced “how gender norms and social practices do not map on to an inherently natural or necessary set of gender norms and relations, but instead function as harmful and oppressive tools for patriarchal control” (Engelstad, 2026, p. 3).

    CR as a Way of Knowing

    Consciousness-raising is more than a method—it offers a different way of knowing. It values lived experience, collective meaning-making, and the knowledge that emerges when people speak, listen, and connect their stories to larger social patterns.

    Scholars have examined CR as an epistemological practice—in other words, a method concerned with how knowledge is produced and justified. When participants discover that seemingly personal experiences recur systematically, they acquire evidence that explanations located solely within individuals may be insufficient. In this way, “consciousness-raising is recognised as epistemically forceful in a range of philosophical literature” (Engelstad, 2026, p. 3).


    In CR, lived experience is a valid and vital form of evidence. Personal stories are not seen as merely anecdotal, but as data that can reveal broader social patterns when shared and analyzed collectively.

    CR challenged the assumption that only credentialed experts—historically men—could produce legitimate knowledge about women’s lives. By treating ordinary women as capable analysts of their own conditions, CR redistributed epistemic authority: the people living an experience were positioned as those best placed to investigate and explain it.


    This raises a question that feminist epistemologists continue to pursue—who is recognized as a credible knower, and whose testimony is dismissed before it can be heard?

    In CR, knowledge was produced collectively rather than handed down. Individual testimony became knowledge through a social process—shared, compared, and tested against others’ accounts until recurring patterns emerged. What first looked like private, idiosyncratic experience was reinterpreted as evidence of shared social conditions.


    Knowledge, in this view, is not discovered by a detached, outside observer, but built together by the people reflecting on and theorizing from their own lives.

    Sometimes people undergo an experience but lack the concepts needed to name or make sense of it. In Epistemic Injustice, philosopher Miranda Fricker (2007) shows how marginalized groups often lack the shared interpretive resources—the “hermeneutic tools”—to understand and express the ways in which they are being wronged, and she argues that this gap is itself an injustice, which she terms hermeneutical injustice.


    CR can be understood as a direct response. By naming shared experiences collectively, participants generated the very concepts—for example, recognizing unequal domestic labor as a political arrangement rather than a personal failing—that allowed them to understand and describe their own situations.

    CR’s way of knowing anticipates methods later formalized in qualitative social research. Like autoethnography, it treats personal, lived experience as a legitimate source of data and analysis rather than mere anecdote.


    Like grounded theory, it builds explanation inductively—beginning with concrete accounts and allowing patterns and theory to emerge from them, rather than imposing an existing framework. Seen this way, CR was not only a political practice but an early form of participatory, experience-grounded inquiry.

    Tensions & critiques

    CR was not without its critics. Two tensions in particular have shaped how the practice is understood: whether it was really just a form of therapy, and whose experiences it centered.

    Outsiders to the practice sometimes dismissed CR as merely a form of psychotherapy, but Sarachild was insistent that it should not be reduced to that term. She acknowledged that CR and psychotherapy might superficially resemble one another, given that both involved small groups, intimate disclosures, and discussions of emotions and personal challenges.


    The difference lay in the question each asked. Whereas therapy might ask how an individual woman could change her perceptions, beliefs, and behaviors, CR instead asked whether a recurring personal difficulty revealed something about the larger social organization of women’s lives.

    Second-wave feminist organizing has been criticized for universalizing experiences particularly associated with white, middle-class women, and CR groups were certainly subject to this same critique. The underlying argument is that the category women does not describe a homogeneous social group: women’s experiences are shaped simultaneously by race, class, sexuality, disability, nationality, and other dimensions of social life.


    In her paper “The Personal Is Epistemological: The Epistemic Force of Consciousness-Raising,” Engelstad (2026) argues that an intersectional CR can investigate differences as well as similarities. Rather than asking “What experience do all women share?” it asks “What patterns and differences emerge when these experiences are compared, and what social conditions might explain them?”
